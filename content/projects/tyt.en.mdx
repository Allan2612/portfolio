## The problem

TyT Tecnología repairs computers, installs security cameras and sells equipment in Pérez Zeledón. It had no place of its own on the web: nothing that showed up when someone searched for the shop, nowhere to see its services, and no catalog to browse.

Three constraints shaped the project:

- **People buy over WhatsApp.** The shop’s customers already close every sale in a chat, so the site had to hand them over to WhatsApp, not replace it with a payment gateway.
- **The owners keep it up to date.** Products, prices, photos, services and promotions change often, and none of that could depend on a developer.
- **Prices in colones, with a dollar reference.** Equipment is often priced against the dollar, so the site shows both, with an exchange rate that updates itself.

## What I built

- **Public site in Spanish and English:** a services landing page with one page per service, a catalog with filters and category pages, product pages, success stories, contact with a map, and legal pages.
- **Cart with WhatsApp checkout.** The cart lives in the browser and, once the customer signs in, in their account; the two merge without losing items. Checkout turns the cart into a WhatsApp message to the shop.
- **Customer accounts** with inquiries handled as message threads, so a customer can choose between the web and WhatsApp.
- **Admin dashboard** for products and photos, services, categories, success stories, inquiries and site settings (WhatsApp number, hours, location, social links), so nothing the owners need to change is hard-coded.
- **Daily exchange rate:** a scheduled job updates it, and every price shows in colones with its dollar reference.
- **AI-assisted product entry:** the admin pastes a product sheet from another store and Gemini fills in the form for review.
- **Promotions for social media**, described below.
- **Technical SEO:** per-page metadata, `hreflang`, a sitemap, structured data and statically generated pages.

## Technical decisions

### Ports and adapters

The domain defines interfaces and knows nothing about the database or outside services. WhatsApp, file storage and the exchange-rate source are adapters behind those interfaces.

That paid off when product images moved from Vercel Blob to Cloudflare R2: the code change was **one new adapter**, with no edits to the business logic, plus a one-off copy of the existing images.

### A visual identity without a photo shoot

The shop had a logo and nothing else: no professional photography, no illustrations, no brand guide. Stock photos would have made it look like any other store, so the design is built from what the brand does have:

- **The home hero is built from TyT’s own isotype**, and its shapes carry down the whole home page instead of a generic banner image.
- **The workshop ticket as the design language.** Product, service and contact cards look like repair tickets, with a reference code and a tear-off cut, and product specs read like a data sheet.
- **A type system instead of imagery:** an expanded typeface for headings, a neutral one for body text and a monospace for workshop data such as codes, specs and labels, on a dark palette where one color is reserved for actions and another for status.
- **Consistent product photos:** AI puts the shop’s own photos on the same studio background (see the next section), so the catalog looks like one collection.

### AI that drafts, a person who approves

Promotions are where AI helps the most and can hurt the most, because a wrong price on a flyer is a promise to a customer. So the flow splits the work:

1. The admin picks a product.
2. Gemini prepares studio-style photos and the promotional copy.
3. The admin reviews **photo by photo** and approves or discards each one.
4. **The code draws the flyer**, in 4:5 and 9:16 for social media, with the price and details taken from the database.

Nothing is published without approval, and the model never writes a price.

### Authorization on every action

Every admin screen and every server action checks the administrator role itself. The routing layer also blocks non-admins, but it is not the only lock: if a request reaches an action some other way, the action still refuses it.

### Quality that runs on its own

- **119 test files**: 105 unit test files with Vitest and 14 end-to-end with Playwright, about 635 tests.
- CI on every push: lint, formatting, tests, build and end-to-end.
- `develop` for daily work and `main` for releases, merged by pull request, with a preview deployment for every branch.
- Handoff notes, a roadmap and a QA checklist for every deploy, so someone else can pick the project up.

## Result

- **Live at [tytsoluciones.net](https://www.tytsoluciones.net)**, bilingual, on the shop’s own domain.
- Built so the owners manage the catalog, services and promotions from the dashboard, without a developer.
- 19 data models and 119 test files, by one developer, in about five months.
- The app is containerized, and a move to self-managed infrastructure to lower hosting costs is in progress.

## What I took from it

The hardest part was not the code: it was making a small shop look established with no professional assets, just a logo and a clear idea of how the workshop works. A design system built from the brand’s own shapes and type held up better than stock images would have, and it scales as the catalog grows.
